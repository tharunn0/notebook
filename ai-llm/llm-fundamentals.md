# Large Language Model Fundamentals: Key Concepts & Mechanics

A foundational guide covering the concepts specific to large language models: tokenization, embeddings, the pretraining/fine-tuning/alignment pipeline, and inference-time behavior.

---

## What is tokenization, and how do subword schemes like BPE work?

Tokenization is the process of converting raw input text into a sequence of discrete integer IDs from a fixed vocabulary that a language model can consume as input, since neural networks operate on numeric tensors, not characters or words directly. The choice of tokenization scheme fundamentally shapes a model's vocabulary size, its sequence length for a given piece of text, and its ability to handle rare or unseen words.

Word-level tokenization (splitting on whitespace/punctuation) is the most intuitive scheme but produces enormous vocabularies and cannot represent any word not seen during vocabulary construction—an out-of-vocabulary word is simply unrepresentable. Character-level tokenization solves the out-of-vocabulary problem entirely, since any string is just a sequence of known characters, but produces very long token sequences for a given piece of text, which is costly given attention's quadratic scaling with sequence length. Subword tokenization, the dominant approach in modern LLMs, sits between these extremes: Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) starts with a base vocabulary of individual characters (or bytes) and iteratively merges the most frequently co-occurring adjacent pair of tokens into a new single token, repeating this process until the vocabulary reaches a target size (commonly 32k–100k+ tokens). The result is a vocabulary where common whole words become single tokens (`"the"`), moderately common words become a small number of subword pieces, and rare or novel words decompose gracefully into smaller, still-meaningful fragments or, in the byte-level BPE variant used by GPT-family models, ultimately down to raw bytes—guaranteeing that literally any input string, including unseen Unicode or code, is representable without an unknown-token fallback.

In production, tokenization has real, sometimes surprising consequences: a model's effective context window is measured in tokens, not characters or words, and languages or domains (code, non-English text) that the tokenizer's training corpus under-represented get split into disproportionately more tokens per unit of meaning, directly inflating cost and consuming context budget for those inputs. Senior engineers account for this when estimating API costs, designing prompts (a prompt engineered around English-centric token boundaries may behave differently once translated), and debugging odd model behavior on numbers, whitespace-sensitive code, or rare tokens—BPE's greedy merge-frequency construction means a model can have systematically weaker representations for tokens that were rare in its tokenizer's training data, independent of how much it saw them during pretraining proper.

## What is an embedding, and how do embedding spaces capture semantic similarity?

An embedding is a dense, learned vector representation of a discrete object—a token, a word, an image, a sentence—that maps it into a continuous, typically high-dimensional (hundreds to thousands of dimensions) numeric space where geometric relationships between vectors correspond to semantic relationships between the objects they represent.

Mechanically, in a language model, an embedding layer is just a lookup table: a matrix of shape `(vocabulary_size, embedding_dimension)`, where each token ID indexes a row of learned weights. These weights are learned the same way any other parameter is—via gradient descent and backpropagation on whatever objective the model is trained on (next-token prediction for an LLM, a contrastive objective for a dedicated embedding model)—and the training process pushes tokens or texts that appear in similar contexts toward similar vectors, since the model finds it useful to treat them similarly for prediction purposes. This is the distributional hypothesis made concrete: "a word is characterized by the company it keeps." The resulting geometry is often strikingly structured—classic word2vec embeddings famously supported analogy arithmetic like `king - man + woman ≈ queen`—and similarity between two embeddings is typically measured via cosine similarity (the angle between the vectors, ignoring magnitude) or dot product/Euclidean distance depending on how the embedding space was trained and normalized.

Beyond the token-embedding lookup table inside a transformer, standalone embedding models (trained with contrastive objectives to pull semantically similar texts together and push dissimilar texts apart) are a foundational production primitive: they power semantic search, recommendation, deduplication, and clustering, and are the retrieval backbone of Retrieval-Augmented Generation, where a query's embedding is compared against a vector database of pre-computed document embeddings to find the most relevant context to feed an LLM. Senior engineers must be careful about embedding model compatibility (vectors from different models or even different versions of the same model generally aren't comparable in the same space), the trade-off between embedding dimensionality (higher dimensions capture more nuance but cost more storage/compute per comparison) and retrieval quality, and the fact that cosine similarity is a proxy for semantic relatedness, not a guarantee—embedding-based retrieval can surface topically similar but factually irrelevant or even contradictory text, which downstream RAG pipelines must account for with reranking or filtering.

## What is the difference between pretraining and fine-tuning?

Pretraining is the initial, most compute-intensive phase of building a large language model, where the model learns general language structure, world knowledge, and reasoning patterns from a massive, broad, largely unlabeled text corpus using a simple self-supervised objective—almost always next-token prediction. Fine-tuning is a subsequent, far cheaper phase that takes an already-pretrained model's weights as a starting point and continues training it on a smaller, more targeted dataset to specialize its behavior for a particular task, domain, or interaction style.

Mechanically, pretraining requires no human-labeled data at all: the "label" for predicting the next token is simply the next token that actually occurs in the training corpus, which is why the objective can scale to trillions of tokens scraped from the web, books, and code—the supervision signal is free, generated automatically from raw text. This phase is where the overwhelming majority of a model's compute budget, parameter count utility, and raw knowledge/capability originate. Fine-tuning, by contrast, typically uses a much smaller (thousands to low millions of examples), often curated or human-authored dataset, and adjusts the pretrained weights—either all of them (full fine-tuning) or a small injected subset (parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like LoRA, which freeze the original weights and train small low-rank adapter matrices instead, dramatically reducing memory and compute cost)—to shift the model's behavior toward a target format, such as following instructions, adopting a conversational assistant persona (supervised fine-tuning, SFT), or excelling at a narrow domain task (e.g., legal document classification, customer support tone).

The practical trade-off senior engineers weigh is fine-tuning versus prompting/RAG for a given use case: fine-tuning is justified when the desired behavior is a durable, hard-to-articulate style or skill shift that would be expensive to re-specify in every prompt (consistent tone, a fixed output schema, domain-specific jargon), whereas injecting facts or context is almost always better handled via prompting or retrieval, since fine-tuning is a comparatively poor and expensive mechanism for teaching a model new factual knowledge—it tends to be forgotten, distorted, or overridden by the vastly larger pretraining distribution unless the fine-tuning dataset is large and repeated, at which point catastrophic forgetting of other pretrained capabilities becomes a real risk that must be guarded against with careful learning-rate selection and mixed-in general data.

## What is RLHF, and why is it used to align LLMs?

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a training technique that further tunes a pretrained (and typically instruction-fine-tuned) language model to better match human preferences—helpfulness, harmlessness, honesty, style—by optimizing the model against a learned reward signal derived from human judgments, rather than against a fixed, hand-labeled correct-answer dataset as in supervised fine-tuning.

Mechanically, RLHF proceeds in stages. First, human annotators compare multiple model-generated responses to the same prompt and rank or choose the better one, producing a preference dataset. This preference data is used to train a separate reward model that takes a (prompt, response) pair and outputs a scalar score predicting how much a human would prefer that response—effectively distilling the fuzzy, expensive-to-scale signal of human judgment into a differentiable function that can be queried cheaply and at scale. The language model is then fine-tuned using a reinforcement learning algorithm—historically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—where the model generates responses, the reward model scores them, and the model's weights are updated to increase the probability of high-reward outputs, typically with a KL-divergence penalty against the original pretrained/SFT model to prevent the policy from drifting so far that it degenerates into reward-hacking gibberish that scores well on the reward model but is actually low quality. More recent techniques like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) achieve a similar alignment effect without training a separate reward model or running full RL, by directly optimizing the language model on the preference pairs with a loss function mathematically derived to have the same optimum as the RLHF objective, which is simpler to implement and tune.

The reason RLHF (or DPO) is necessary rather than optional is that next-token prediction on internet text, the pretraining objective, optimizes for predicting plausible human-written continuations, not for being a helpful, safe, instruction-following assistant—those are emergent side effects at best. RLHF closes that gap by directly optimizing for what humans actually prefer in an assistant's response, which is why it is credited with much of the qualitative leap between a raw pretrained base model (which tends to ramble, complete a prompt rather than answer it, and lacks a consistent persona) and a production chat assistant. The trade-off senior engineers must manage is that the reward model is itself an imperfect proxy for true human preference, trained on a necessarily limited and sometimes inconsistent labeling dataset, so aggressive RL optimization against it can produce reward hacking (sycophancy, verbosity, or superficially agreeable but low-substance answers that score well on the proxy without reflecting genuine quality)—a phenomenon actively monitored and mitigated through reward model ensembling, KL penalties, and increasingly sophisticated preference data collection.

## What is a context window, and what limits it?

A context window is the maximum number of tokens—spanning the prompt, any retrieved or injected context, and the model's own generated output—that a language model can attend to and condition its predictions on in a single forward pass; any content beyond this limit is either truncated or simply invisible to the model.

Mechanically, the context window is constrained by two compounding factors. First, self-attention's compute cost scales quadratically with sequence length, since every token must compute a compatibility score against every other token—doubling the context length roughly quadruples the attention compute for a given layer, which historically made very long contexts prohibitively expensive to train and serve. Second, and increasingly the dominant constraint in practice, is memory: autoregressive decoding requires caching the Key and Value vectors for every previous token in the sequence (the KV cache) so they don't need to be recomputed at every new generation step, and this cache grows linearly with context length and with the number of attention heads/layers, consuming GPU memory that would otherwise serve other requests or a larger batch size—at long context lengths, the KV cache can dwarf the memory footprint of the model's weights themselves. Positional encoding schemes also historically imposed a hard ceiling: a model trained only on sequences up to some length using absolute positional embeddings has no learned representation for positions beyond that length, though modern relative schemes like RoPE, combined with techniques like position interpolation/extrapolation, allow context windows to be extended well beyond what was seen during the bulk of pretraining.

In production, a long context window does not guarantee a model uses all of it effectively—the well-documented "lost in the middle" phenomenon shows that models tend to attend more reliably to information near the start and end of a long context than to content buried in the middle, so senior engineers still structure prompts to place the most critical information at the edges of the context even when the window technically has room to spare. Long-context inference is also substantially more expensive and higher-latency per request than short-context inference due to the attention and KV-cache costs above, which is precisely the engineering motivation behind Retrieval-Augmented Generation: rather than stuffing an entire knowledge base into the context window on every request, RAG retrieves only the most relevant slice of it, keeping the effective context small, fast, and cheap while still giving the model access to a much larger total corpus of information than its window could ever hold at once.

## What are temperature, top-k, and top-p sampling in text generation?

Temperature, top-k, and top-p are decoding-time hyperparameters that control how a language model selects the next token from its predicted probability distribution over the vocabulary, trading off between deterministic, high-confidence output and diverse, creative output—none of them change the model's weights or its underlying predicted probabilities; they only reshape how those probabilities are sampled from.

At each generation step, the model produces a raw score (logit) for every token in the vocabulary, and a softmax converts these into a probability distribution. Temperature rescales the logits before the softmax is applied (`logits / temperature`): a temperature below 1 sharpens the distribution, concentrating probability mass on the already-highest-scoring tokens and pushing the output toward the deterministic, most-likely continuation (temperature approaching 0 becomes equivalent to greedy decoding—always picking the single highest-probability token), while a temperature above 1 flattens the distribution, giving lower-probability tokens a meaningfully higher chance of being sampled and producing more varied, sometimes less coherent output. Top-k sampling restricts sampling to only the `k` highest-probability tokens at each step, zeroing out the probability of every other token before sampling, which prevents the model from ever selecting a wildly implausible token even under high temperature, but uses a fixed cutoff regardless of how the actual distribution is shaped—problematic when the distribution is very peaked (k tokens may include implausible ones) or very flat (k tokens may exclude reasonable ones). Top-p (nucleus) sampling addresses this by instead selecting the smallest set of top tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds a threshold `p`, so the number of candidate tokens adapts dynamically to the shape of the distribution at each step—narrow when the model is confident, wider when it is uncertain.

In production, these parameters are tuned per use case rather than left at a single default: low temperature (often near 0) with greedy or narrow top-p is standard for deterministic tasks like code generation, structured data extraction, or factual Q&A where consistency and correctness matter more than variety, while higher temperature and wider top-p/top-k are used for creative writing, brainstorming, or generating diverse candidates for downstream reranking. Senior engineers also account for the interaction between these settings and reproducibility—since sampling is stochastic by design, any temperature above 0 means identical prompts can yield different outputs across calls, which matters for testing, caching, and evaluation pipelines that assume determinism unless a fixed random seed is also pinned (and even seeded determinism is not always guaranteed across different hardware/batching conditions in production serving stacks).

## What is hallucination in LLMs, and why does it occur?

Hallucination refers to a language model generating text that is fluent, confident, and plausible-sounding but factually incorrect, fabricated, or unsupported by any real source—the model states something false with the same syntactic confidence it would use for a true statement, because nothing in its architecture or training objective directly represents or checks factual grounding.

Mechanically, hallucination is a near-inevitable consequence of what an LLM is actually optimized to do: next-token prediction trains the model to produce the statistically most plausible continuation of a sequence given its training distribution, not to produce verified true statements—plausibility and truth are correlated in the training data but not identical, and the model has no built-in mechanism to distinguish "this pattern is common in my training data" from "this is actually true." Hallucination is compounded by several specific failure modes: knowledge gaps, where the model is asked about facts sparsely or never represented in its training data and it interpolates a plausible-sounding but fabricated answer rather than expressing uncertainty; knowledge cutoff, where the model confidently reports stale information as current because it has no awareness that time has passed since training; and compounding errors during long autoregressive generation, where an early fabricated token becomes part of the context for every subsequent token, and the model, conditioned on its own prior (false) output, continues building a coherent-sounding narrative around it rather than self-correcting—since each token is generated to be locally plausible given everything generated so far, not globally fact-checked against reality.

Mitigating hallucination in production is an active, multi-layered engineering problem rather than a solved one: Retrieval-Augmented Generation grounds generation in retrieved source documents and instructs the model to answer only from that provided context, substantially reducing (though not eliminating) fabrication on knowledge-intensive queries; prompting techniques that explicitly permit and reward the model for saying "I don't know" reduce the pressure toward confident fabrication; lower-temperature decoding reduces variance-driven fabrication; and post-hoc verification—citation requirements, fact-checking against retrieved sources, or a second model/pass that scores the response's grounding—catches errors that generation-time mitigations miss. RLHF and preference-tuning also shape hallucination behavior, for better or worse: reward models that favor confident, complete-sounding answers over hedged uncertainty can inadvertently train models toward more, not less, confident hallucination, which is why calibration (a model's stated confidence actually matching its accuracy) is treated as a distinct alignment target from raw helpfulness.

## What is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and how does it mitigate hallucination and knowledge cutoff?

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is an architecture pattern that couples a language model with an external retrieval system: at query time, relevant documents or passages are fetched from a knowledge base (often a vector database of pre-computed embeddings) and injected into the model's context window alongside the user's query, so the model generates its answer grounded in that retrieved, verifiable content rather than relying solely on facts implicitly memorized during pretraining.

Mechanically, a typical RAG pipeline has three stages. First, an ingestion/indexing stage chunks a corpus of documents into passages of a manageable size, computes an embedding vector for each chunk using an embedding model, and stores those vectors (along with the source text) in a vector database that supports efficient approximate nearest-neighbor search. Second, at query time, the user's query is embedded with the same embedding model, and the vector database returns the top-k most semantically similar chunks by cosine similarity or another distance metric—this retrieval step is often followed by a reranking stage using a more expensive but more accurate cross-encoder model to reorder the initial candidates by finer-grained relevance, since fast vector similarity search trades some precision for speed. Third, the retrieved chunks are formatted into the LLM's prompt (typically with an instruction to answer using only the provided context and to acknowledge when the answer isn't present), and the model generates its response conditioned on this injected, current, verifiable evidence rather than purely on parametric memory baked into its weights during training.

RAG mitigates hallucination and knowledge cutoff by shifting the source of truth from the model's opaque, frozen internal weights to explicit, inspectable, and updatable external documents: the knowledge base can be updated continuously without retraining the model at all, directly solving the knowledge-cutoff problem, and because the model is instructed to ground its answer in the provided passages, a well-designed RAG system also enables citation—pointing the user to the exact source text an answer was derived from—which lets both the model and the end user verify claims rather than trusting the model's fluency alone. The trade-offs senior engineers manage in production RAG systems include retrieval quality (irrelevant or poorly-chunked retrieved context can actively mislead the model, sometimes producing worse answers than no retrieval at all), the added latency and infrastructure cost of the retrieval and reranking stages, chunking strategy (chunks too small lose context, chunks too large dilute relevance and waste context-window budget), and the residual risk that a model still hallucinates even with correct context in front of it if it fails to properly attend to or prioritize the retrieved passages over its own parametric priors.

## What is in-context learning, and how does it differ from fine-tuning?

In-context learning is the ability of a large language model to adapt its behavior on a new task purely from examples or instructions provided within the prompt at inference time—with zero updates to the model's weights—simply by conditioning its next-token predictions on the demonstrations given in the context window. Zero-shot prompting gives only an instruction, one-shot gives a single example, and few-shot gives several examples of the desired input-output pattern before the actual query.

Mechanically, this capability is an emergent property of large-scale next-token pretraining rather than something explicitly engineered: because the pretraining corpus contains vast amounts of text where a pattern is established and then continued or applied (structured documents, Q&A pairs, tutorials, code with consistent style), a sufficiently large model trained on next-token prediction implicitly learns to recognize "the task being demonstrated in this context" as a signal and to continue generating in a way consistent with it—the model's attention mechanism lets it directly reference the provided examples' input-output structure when predicting the next tokens of a new query, effectively performing task inference and application within a single forward pass rather than through any gradient update. Research on this phenomenon (framing it as the model implicitly performing something functionally similar to a small optimization step encoded in its forward pass, sometimes described as "meta-learning") shows this capability scales strongly with model size—smaller models benefit far less from few-shot examples than large ones, which is one of the more striking emergent capabilities separating small and frontier-scale LLMs.

The practical distinction from fine-tuning is stark: in-context learning requires no training infrastructure, no labeled dataset beyond a handful of examples, and adapts instantly per-request, but its effect is entirely transient—it vanishes the moment the examples leave the context window, consumes context-window budget on every single request (adding latency and cost), and is fundamentally capped by how much behavior can be conveyed through demonstration alone versus a genuine, durable weight update. Senior engineers reach for in-context learning/prompting first for its speed of iteration and zero infrastructure cost, escalate to RAG when the limiting factor is missing knowledge rather than missing skill, and escalate further to fine-tuning only when a task requires a persistent behavioral or stylistic shift that few-shot examples can't reliably or economically induce on every single request—these three techniques are complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and production LLM systems frequently combine all three.
